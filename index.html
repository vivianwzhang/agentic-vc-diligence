01 The problem

I source and evaluate 200+ companies a year. Every deal touches the same work streams: product, market, traction, team, competition, financials, contracts, cap table, sales pipeline. Running each from scratch was the largest fixed cost in my week, so I agentified the repetitive part, freeing time for thesis work (including one I've built in RL) and founder relationships.

02 How I built it

I mapped the full workflow on Miro first, every step from first meeting to IC, before writing a single prompt. Then I trained each agent against real data rooms, comparing its output to work I'd already done and correcting it the way I'd train a new analyst.

03 Stage 1: Screening

A deal enters the pipeline the moment we complete a first meeting and get data room access. Five agents run in parallel on that data room, each producing a structured overview: Product, Market, Traction, Team, and a basic Competitive Scan. A sixth agent, the Pass Level 1 recommender, reads all five outputs and returns a go or no-go call in about a week, instead of a partner spending a day skimming the data room cold.

If we pass on the deal but the theme is one I want to keep tracking, the same research routes into a thesis or market report and gets archived. The next founder who crosses my desk in that space starts from an existing base instead of a blank page.

Level 1 agents

  • Product Overview · what the company builds
  • Market Overview · where it sits and how big
  • Traction Overview · revenue, growth, customers
  • Team Overview · founders and key hires
  • Basic Competitive Scan · who else is in the space
  • Pass Level 1 Recommender · synthesizes the five into a go/no-go

The actual agent prompts

The summaries below are a short synthesis of the real system prompts, which live in the Notion doc where they're maintained (AI — Screening Sub-Agent Prompts), condensed here and scrubbed of internal workspace references. Click to expand.

product-screener

Runs a fast, evidence-grounded first pass on product and technology. Reads the data room's technical strategy doc and demo materials first, strips marketing language, then cross-checks against external sources: technical blog posts, founder interviews, published benchmarks.

  • Core questions: what's the technical innovation, is there proprietary IP, is an AI product's core model proprietary or a wrapper, what would take a competitor 12+ months to replicate.
  • Verdict scale: STRONG (moat visible, 12+ months to replicate) / MONITOR (thin or early differentiation) / WEAK (no differentiation, unsubstantiated claims).
  • Hard stop: an AI product that's a thin wrapper on a commodity model with no data advantage.
market-screener

Builds an independent view of market size and timing rather than anchoring on the founder's own TAM slide. Cross-checks against industry reports, comparable funding rounds, and regulatory shifts.

  • Core questions: honest SAM with methodology, real growth rate, the specific "why now," whether this is a new-category play or a share-take from incumbents.
  • Verdict scale: STRONG ($1B+ SAM, >20% growth, clear why-now) / MONITOR (sizing uncertain or timing weak) / WEAK (SAM under $300M, no timing catalyst).
  • Hard stop: a market a well-capitalized public company already dominates with no visible wedge.
traction-screener

Skeptical by default: pilots are not ARR, LOIs are not customers. Reads the financial model and MIS, then applies stage-appropriate benchmarks (e.g. seed expects $100k–$500k ARR or 3–5 paying customers with visible month-over-month growth).

  • Core questions: contracted revenue vs. pipeline, customer concentration, NRR, burn and runway, whether traction matches the pitch or reveals a gap.
  • Verdict scale: STRONG (ahead of stage, real growth) / MONITOR (on-track but revenue quality or concentration in question) / WEAK (mostly pipeline/LOIs, flat growth).
  • Hard stop: ARR that includes non-recurring components inflated to look recurring.
team-screener

A public-sources-only pass (back-channel references are saved for full DD). Builds an independent view of each founder rather than relying on the self-reported bio, then cross-checks dates and companies against LinkedIn and press.

  • Core questions: domain expertise with evidence, technical credibility, prior track record, discrepancies between the stated bio and what's publicly verifiable, critical team gaps for the next 18 months.
  • Verdict scale: STRONG (domain expertise + technical credibility + no discrepancies) / MONITOR (gaps manageable with hiring) / WEAK (no relevant background in a specialized space, or credential discrepancies).
  • Hard stop: a credential discrepancy that can't be explained, or identifiable prior fraud.
competitive-screener

Maps direct competitors, adjacent players, and the incumbent solution customers use today, then stress-tests the founder's differentiation claim against each one. "No real competitors" is treated as a red flag, not a green one.

  • Core questions: who's best-funded, what would it take a leading competitor to replicate the core advantage, does the moat strengthen or weaken with scale, is the market winner-take-most or fragmented.
  • Verdict scale: STRONG (clear, defensible differentiation, low switching cost to win) / MONITOR (thin differentiation, crowded field) / WEAK (undifferentiated against well-funded leaders).
  • Hard stop: a 10x-better-funded direct competitor has already shipped an equivalent product.
pass-level-1

Reads all 5 briefs and makes an opinionated PASS / FAIL / BORDERLINE call, not an average of scores. Runs on an explicit weighting framework: what can compensate for what, and what's an automatic fail regardless of everything else.

  • Compensating signals: an exceptional team can offset weak traction at pre-seed/seed; strong traction can offset a crowded competitive field; a strong market with a real "why now" can offset early product.
  • Automatic fails: any hard stop flagged by any screener, WEAK on both Team and Product, or traction claims with evidence of manipulation.
  • BORDERLINE is reserved for a deal with one specific open question that, if answered, would flip it to PASS, named explicitly rather than left vague.

04 Stage 2: Deep diligence

A deal that clears Level 1 hands off to a shared research repository that seven specialist agents read from and write into. Each one owns a full work stream and produces its findings independently before they get synthesized together.

Level 2 agents

  • Market Sizing · independent TAM/SAM/SOM, not the founder's own numbers
  • Competition Mapping · landscape, feature comparison, supply chain dynamics
  • Founder Due Diligence · prior companies, outcomes, references, red flags
  • Sales Pipeline Analysis · splits real ARR from POs and LOIs, flags plateaus
  • Contract Review · IP assignment, vesting, exclusivity, change-of-control
  • Financial Analysis · P&L, unit economics, Rule of 40, burn multiple
  • Cap Table Review · dilution trajectory, founder ownership, ESOP runway

Each is a full research process on its own. I haven't included these as downloads, but I'm happy to walk through any of them live.

Exa — Pass L1 BORDERLINE (demo)

Workstream verdicts summary

ProductSTRONG — purpose-built retrieval for machine consumption, moat plausible but not independently benchmarked.
MarketSTRONG — real tailwind (agent adoption), but TAM/SAM not independently sized in this pass.
TractionMONITOR — 5,000+ logos is real signal, but no public ARR, growth, or concentration data.
TeamSTRONG — four years on one thesis, high-quality investor syndicate across three rounds.
CompetitionMONITOR — a real, well-funded competitor (Perplexity) contesting the same wedge.

Top 3 reasons for

  1. Logo growth to 5,000+ companies, including credible developer-tooling names (Cursor, Cognition), suggests genuine usage, not just funding-driven hype.
  2. Founders have run one thesis for four years through a rebrand and three funding rounds, a longer conviction runway than most AI-native peers.
  3. A top-tier investor syndicate (YC, Lightspeed, Benchmark, a16z) across multiple rounds is repeated third-party validation, not one fund's opinion.

Top 3 reasons against

  1. No public revenue, growth, or retention figures exist to confirm the traction is real rather than a logo-count vanity metric.
  2. At a $2.2B valuation this deal sits well outside my fund's pre-seed to Series B mandate regardless of workstream quality.
  3. Perplexity is a well-funded, direct competitor already contesting the same agent-facing search wedge.

Hard stops identified

None from this screen. The stage/valuation mismatch with my fund's mandate is a fund-fit issue, not a hard stop under this rubric.

What has to be true for this to work

  1. The 5,000+ logo count converts to durable, growing paid revenue rather than free-tier signups.
  2. Exa's retrieval quality stays differentiated enough that Google or Perplexity can't close the gap with existing infrastructure.
  3. The category keeps growing faster than incumbents can react.

Open items

  • What is actual ARR and net revenue retention? Public sources give no figure.
  • What share of the 5,000+ logos are paying at meaningful volume vs. trial/free tier?

Recommendation rationale

On workstream merit alone, Exa reads as a strong company: a team that has held one thesis for four years, a product built specifically for a fast-growing category, and an investor syndicate that has repeatedly re-underwritten the business at higher prices. That combination is what a STRONG/STRONG/STRONG screen across Product, Market, and Team should produce.

The honest gap is Traction. Every figure that would normally confirm real commercial scale, ARR, growth rate, concentration, is absent from public sources, and this screen doesn't fill that gap with a guess. Logo count is a real but partial signal. Until a real data room resolves it, this stays BORDERLINE rather than PASS on the workstreams.

Separate from the screen itself: at a $2.2B valuation this is a growth-stage check, well outside my fund's pre-seed to Series B mandate. In a live pipeline this would likely be archived to a theme file rather than pursued as an active deal, not because the workstreams are weak, but because the fund doesn't write this size of check.
